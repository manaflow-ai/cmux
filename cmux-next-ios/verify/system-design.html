How the phone reaches your Mac

Your iPhone never talks to your Mac through a server that sees your terminals. A small cloud service introduces the two devices, then they open an encrypted link straight to each other, or through a relay when the network forces it. Everything you do in the app travels inside that link.

The whole picture

Three groups of parts. The iPhone app. The cmux-next Mac app, which runs a helper called cmux-mobile-host that answers the phone. And a small cloud service on Cloudflare that handles accounts, presence and introductions. Your terminal contents, files and agent chats go only over the device-to-device link.

Two separate cloud deployments exist: a production service (mobile-api.cmux.dev, its own database) that accepts only production accounts, and a dev service for testing. Your phone build and Mac build both use production.

Accounts and who may connect

Sign-in is Stack Auth, the same account system cmux uses. Every request to the cloud service carries your sign-in token, and the service checks it before doing anything.

  • Your phone can only connect to Macs you signed in on yourself. Teammates can see each other's team, but in this version nobody can dial someone else's Mac.
  • Teams and the personal space. A team comes from Stack. If you have no team, you get a personal space that only you can use. Each Mac is registered into one of them.
  • Devices. Each phone and Mac makes up its own device id on first launch and keeps it in the Keychain. The cloud keeps a row per device: kind, name, team, last seen, and the phone's push token.

Finding your Mac

Each team has one TeamHub, a small always-addressable object in Cloudflare that keeps a WebSocket open to every online device in the team. It plays three roles: it tells the phone which Macs are online, it passes the introduction messages between phone and Mac, and it can relay traffic as a last resort.

  1. 1The Mac app, with Settings → Mobile → Allow iPhone Connections on, starts cmux-mobile-host. It registers the Mac and keeps its TeamHub socket open.
  2. 2The phone signs in and makes one bootstrap call that registers it and returns your Macs and the relay credentials in a single round trip.
  3. 3The phone opens its own TeamHub socket at launch and keeps it warm, so a later dial starts immediately. Presence updates (online, last seen, removed) arrive on it.

Connecting

To connect, the phone sends an introduction through the TeamHub. The hub only forwards it if the target Mac belongs to the same user, and stamps who sent it so the Mac can check too. The two devices then build an encrypted link directly. Today there are two ways to build it, chosen in Settings → Connection → Transport.

WebRTC (default today)

  • Finds a path with ICE: tries your local network, then your public address, then the relay.
  • Encrypted with DTLS. Carries the link in data channels.
  • Relay: Cloudflare TURN.

WireGuard

  • An encrypted tunnel inside the app (no VPN profile). Each side has a key pair; the introduction swaps public keys and addresses.
  • The link runs as TCP streams inside the tunnel.
  • Relay: Cloudflare TURN, or the TeamHub itself over a secure WebSocket on port 443 when UDP is blocked.

The path ladder

Whichever transport, the phone takes the best path that works and falls back down the ladder:

  1. 1Direct. Phone and Mac exchange packets with no middleman. Fastest; used on the same Wi-Fi and on most home networks.
  2. 2TURN relay. When the networks won't let packets through directly, both send to a nearby Cloudflare TURN server that forwards them. Still end-to-end encrypted; the relay sees only scrambled packets. Measured about 16 ms round trip.
  3. 3TeamHub relay (WireGuard only). When even UDP is blocked, tunnel packets ride the hub's WebSocket on port 443. Slowest (hundreds of ms), but it gets through nearly any network.

The link sends a heartbeat every 15 s and gives up only after 20 s of total silence. Typing has its own lane so it never waits behind output (next section).

Inside the link

Once connected, everything is one encrypted link, split into numbered channels. Each channel talks to one service on the Mac. cmux-mobile-host refuses anything not on its list, and filters what each service may do.

Two lanes

A single stream would make a keystroke wait behind megabytes of output. So the link has a main lane for bulk data and an input lane for keystrokes and ^C. Under a heavy output flood, typing echo stays around 15 ms.

Trust and approvals

A stolen sign-in token is not enough to reach your Mac. Three checks sit on top of sign-in:

  1. 1Pair once on the Mac. The first time a phone connects, the Mac shows "Allow “iPhone” to connect?" inside the cmux window, with a notification and a Dock bounce. Only your own click counts. The phone keeps its keys in the Secure Enclave; the Mac remembers the approved key.
  2. 2Face ID for anything that grants. Allowing an agent's action, or answering a question, is signed by the phone after Face ID. The signature covers that one prompt and answer, on that Mac, for 5 minutes, once. Deny needs no signature.
  3. 3The phone pins the Mac. The Mac proves a long-lived identity key while pairing. If it ever changes, the phone refuses to connect and tells you.

Removing a device from either side ends its live link at once and keeps it off until you allow it again.

Terminals

Terminals are sent as text, not video. The Mac's terminal daemon streams the raw terminal output; the phone runs its own Ghostty engine and draws it locally. That keeps text sharp at any size, gives real scrollback, copy and search, and uses very little data.

  • Attaching replays the Mac's history (up to about 2 MB of text), then streams live output.
  • Typing goes up the input lane. The composer sends a whole draft as one paste, then Return.
  • Size: the phone tells the Mac its size, so full-screen programs like top and Codex lay out for the phone.

Browser tabs

A browser tab can't be sent as text, so the Mac renders the page in a browser host and streams it as H.264 video, encoded by the Mac's hardware and decoded by the phone's. Your taps, scrolls and typing go back over the link.

Notifications

When an agent needs you, the Mac tells the cloud service, which sends an Apple push to your phone. The notification names the agent and workspace only, never what the agent wrote. Push works in TestFlight builds; the hand-installed build relies on in-app alerts.

Decided and next

Decided: WireGuard becomes the one network between phone and Mac for everything. Terminals stay text. Video (browser tabs, later whole-screen viewing) rides on top.
